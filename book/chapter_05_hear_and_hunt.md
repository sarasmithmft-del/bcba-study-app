# Chapter 5
## H is for Hear & Hunt - The Intake Reimagined

> *"Most hygienists are trained to clean first and listen second. High-growth hygiene flips that order."*

## The Lesion Nobody Missed and Everybody Missed

Monica was clinically sharp.
Her charting was clean.
Her room turnover was efficient.
Her doctor trusted her eyes.

And she almost missed a lesion.

To be precise: she saw it.
What she nearly missed was what made it urgent.

The patient - I will call him Andre - was 52, regular recare, generally cooperative, no major drama in his chart. Monica's visual exam picked up a suspicious lateral tongue area. Not huge. Not "alarm bells now." But enough to watch closely and likely refer.

In the old workflow, that would have been the whole story:

- note lesion
- tell doctor
- mention "let's keep an eye on this"

What changed this case was one question she asked before she touched an instrument:

> "What has changed for you in the last few months that might be affecting your health - even if it feels unrelated to your mouth?"

Andre paused, then said he had lost thirty pounds unintentionally, had persistent throat irritation, and had started waking up drenched in sweat.

Now the lesion was not just a finding.
It was a finding in context.

That changed urgency, handoff quality, and referral speed.
He was seen quickly by oral medicine. Biopsy was positive. Early intervention window was still open.

Later, Monica told me:

> "I always looked. I did not always hunt."

That sentence is the hinge of this chapter.

Hygienists are not struggling because they cannot see.
They are struggling because intake systems often fail to surface the context that tells you what what you see actually means.

Hear & Hunt fixes that.

---

## Why Traditional Intake Underperforms

Most recare intakes in dentistry follow this sequence:

1. confirm med history updates
2. ask if anything hurts
3. proceed to cleaning workflow

This sequence is efficient.
It is also incomplete.

It misses three high-value realities:

1. Patients often do not know what matters clinically.
2. Patients withhold "unrelated" information unless invited clearly.
3. Patients answer narrow questions narrowly.

If your first question is "Any changes to your health?"
you will often get "No."
Not because no changes exist.
Because the patient's definition of "health change" is not your definition.

Hear & Hunt is not extra conversation for conversation's sake.
It is diagnostic risk reduction through better context gathering.

---

## The Triple Intake

The core framework for this chapter:

**Hear & Hunt = Triple Intake**

1. **Medical Context**
2. **Social Context**
3. **Oral Context**

Most teams collect Medical and Oral shallowly and skip Social entirely.
That creates blind spots in diagnosis, compliance, scheduling reliability, and treatment planning.

Triple Intake does not require a long interview.
It requires better questions in a better order.

---

## Intake Order Matters More Than Most Teams Realize

Use this order in recare and new-patient hygiene appointments:

1. **Open context question** (30-45 seconds)
2. **Medical context check**
3. **Social stress/context check**
4. **Oral symptom/context check**
5. **Clinical exam and hunt**

Why this order works:

- The patient shifts from passive to participatory early.
- You gather pattern clues before you interpret findings.
- Clinical observations are read against living context, not isolated data points.

This order alone improves the quality of what patients disclose.

## The Three Questions Every Hygienist Should Ask

These are your non-negotiable Hear & Hunt questions.
Use exact wording or close variants that feel natural to your voice.

### Question 1 (Context opener)

> "What has changed for you since I last saw you that might be affecting your health - even if it feels unrelated to your mouth?"

Why it works:

- "what has changed" triggers recall better than "any changes?"
- "even if unrelated" broadens disclosure bandwidth

### Question 2 (Body signal)

> "What is your body telling you lately that you have been ignoring?"

Why it works:

- surfaces fatigue, sleep issues, stress, dry mouth, medication effects
- identifies health behaviors that impact oral outcomes

### Question 3 (Oral concern)

> "If I could fix one thing in your mouth this year that would make daily life easier, what would you pick?"

Why it works:

- translates concern into functional priority
- reveals hidden pain points and motivation anchors

If a hygienist asks these three consistently, hunt quality rises.

---

## Medical Context: Beyond Checkbox Updates

Hygiene teams often treat medical history as compliance formality.
It should be interpreted as risk intelligence.

### Common medical-context misses

- new GLP-1 or diabetes medication effects (dry mouth, GI patterns, eating changes)
- blood pressure med changes affecting oral dryness
- autoimmune flares
- sleep disruption patterns
- oncology treatment timelines

### Better approach

Do not only ask "new meds?"
Ask:

> "What changed about your medications or your daily routine since your last visit?"

Then follow with:

> "What side effect are you noticing most?"

Side effects often explain oral status changes better than the diagnosis label itself.

---

## Social Context: The Most Ignored Diagnostic Variable

Social context is not "small talk."
It is often the hidden driver of oral behavior.

High-yield social factors:

- caregiving load
- job instability
- grief
- sleep loss
- transportation instability
- financial compression windows

These directly affect:

- homecare consistency
- appointment adherence
- decision timing
- stress behaviors (clenching, sugar exposure, neglect cycles)

You are not becoming a therapist.
You are gathering decision-relevant context to calibrate care plans realistically.

---

## Oral Context: Ask Better, Get Better

"Any pain?" is low-yield.

Use targeted oral context prompts:

- "What area in your mouth has gotten your attention recently?"
- "What feels different when you chew?"
- "Any tooth or gum spot you avoid using?"
- "What have you changed in your routine because of your mouth?"

These uncover functional adaptation signals patients do not classify as pain.

Patients often normalize dysfunction.
Hygienists must un-normalize it with better questions.

---

## Hunt Skills: What to Look For Once You Hear

Hunt means integrating context with exam behavior.

After Triple Intake, your clinical hunt should intentionally target:

1. **High-risk surfaces linked to disclosed habits**
2. **Occlusal stress signs linked to sleep/stress report**
3. **Inflammatory patterns linked to systemic context**
4. **Soft tissue areas linked to symptom language**
5. **Behavior-pattern mismatch** (patient reports "excellent brushing" but plaque pattern indicates technique gap)

Hunt is where listening becomes clinical precision.

---

## The "What I Heard, What I Saw" Method

This is the bridge between intake and diagnosis quality.

After each significant encounter, capture two lines:

- **What I heard**
- **What I saw**

Example:

- Heard: new night shifts, poor sleep, jaw tension on waking
- Saw: wear facets increasing, masseter tenderness, fractured cusp risk on #14

This method improves:

- handoff clarity
- future continuity
- mentorship quality

It also prevents the common error of documenting findings without context.

---

## Handoff Quality Starts Here, Not in Chapter 7

Chapter 7 is Invite and handoff choreography.
But handoff quality is prebuilt in Hear & Hunt.

If your intake is shallow, your handoff is shallow.
If your intake is rich, your handoff can be concise and high-value.

Compare:

Weak handoff:
> "Doctor, patient has sensitivity upper right."

Strong handoff:
> "Doctor, Andre reports new nighttime clenching and upper-right chew avoidance for six weeks. I am seeing progressing wear and a cusp risk at #2. We reviewed this on camera and he wants options before it worsens."

Strong handoffs are not longer.
They are denser because Hear & Hunt was done well.

## Common Hear & Hunt Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Checklist Interview Voice

Patients disengage when intake sounds administrative.

Fix:
Use conversational cadence, then document.

### Mistake 2: Asking but Not Pausing

Hygienist asks a good question and fills silence too quickly.

Fix:
Count to three after asking.
Silence is where useful answers emerge.

### Mistake 3: Ignoring "small" disclosures

Patient says "I am not sleeping."
Hygienist says "Got it," then proceeds as usual.

Fix:
Tie disclosure to hunt focus and say so explicitly:
> "Thanks for sharing that. I am going to pay extra attention to pressure patterns and soft tissue dryness today."

### Mistake 4: Over-documenting, under-interpreting

Chart has data, no synthesis.

Fix:
Include one interpretation line linking context to finding.

## Hear & Hunt Under Time Pressure

The common pushback:
"We do not have time for this."

You do, if you remove low-yield language.

Hear & Hunt can be done in 90-150 seconds with discipline.

Replace:

- repeated generic pleasantries
- duplicate administrative questions
- non-actionable verbal loops

with:

- one context opener
- three high-yield questions
- one explicit hunt focus statement

The goal is not longer appointments.
The goal is better signal per minute.

---

## Team Coaching: Installing Hear & Hunt in 30 Days

Use this implementation pattern:

### Week 1

- baseline observation of current intake language
- identify top three weak points

### Week 2

- train the 3-question set
- role-play 10 minutes/day

### Week 3

- add "What I heard, what I saw" documentation line
- audit 5 encounters per hygienist

### Week 4

- calibrate handoff density quality
- compare CCDR and patient "felt heard" comments

This is a behavior system, not a one-time script rollout.

## Metrics to Track for This Chapter

To confirm Hear & Hunt is working, monitor:

- intake completion consistency
- context-linked documentation quality
- CCDR movement
- doctor handoff quality scores
- patient comments indicating "felt heard / known"

Early expected indicators (2-4 weeks):

- better handoff specificity
- fewer "surprise" findings at doctor exam
- more patient questions before doctor enters

Later indicators (4-8 weeks):

- CCDR and SDA upward trend
- fewer stalled treatment discussions

## The Chapter in One Sentence

If your intake process only captures what patients think is relevant, you will miss what actually drives their oral risk and treatment decisions.

Ask better.
Listen longer by a few seconds.
Hunt with context.
Document synthesis.

That is how hygienists move from procedural efficiency to diagnostic leadership.

> **One question to close on:**
>
> *What did your last five patients tell you that changed how you examined them - and if nothing changed, are you hearing enough to hunt well?*

## Tool 5.3 - Handoff Density Template

> *Turn intake into concise, high-value doctor handoffs.*

### 20-Second Handoff Structure

1. **Context signal** (what patient reported)
2. **Clinical signal** (what you found)
3. **Decision orientation** (what patient wants next)

Template:

> "Dr. ______, patient reports ______ over ______ timeframe. I am seeing ______ at ______. We reviewed this and patient wants to discuss ______ options today."

### Quick Quality Check

- [ ] includes patient-reported context
- [ ] includes specific finding + location
- [ ] includes patient orientation to decision
- [ ] avoids vague language ("just sensitive," "take a look")

### Example

> "Dr. Patel, Andre reports six weeks of upper-right chew avoidance and poor sleep from jaw tension. I am seeing cusp risk at #2 and progressing wear facets. We reviewed this on camera and he wants options before this turns urgent."
