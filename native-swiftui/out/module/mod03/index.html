Chapter 3

Selecting and Defining Target Behaviors

READING — Chapter 3Cooper/Heron/Heward

Chapter reading

Chapter 3 positions target-behavior selection as a clinical decision process, not a quick naming step. Before treatment procedures are chosen, the analyst evaluates which behavior changes will improve day-to-day functioning, widen access to naturally occurring reinforcement, and reduce long-term exposure to punishment.

A first filter is habilitation: prioritize targets that increase practical independence and durable quality-of-life outcomes across settings, rather than goals that only satisfy short-term convenience for staff or systems. Analysts then verify social validity by checking whether stakeholders value the selected goals, procedures, and expected outcomes in meaningful routines.

Within selection logic, CHH distinguishes behavioral cusps from pivotal behaviors. A behavioral cusp is a change that opens access to new environments, contingencies, and reinforcer communities that were previously unavailable to the learner. A pivotal behavior, by contrast, is a strategic target whose improvement produces collateral gains across untreated response classes without separately training each one.

After selecting what to change, analysts must define behavior in observable, objective, and complete terms. Strong operational definitions specify what counts, what does not count, and when a response begins and ends so independent observers can agree on the same events. CHH also ties this definition phase to front-end information gathering (record review, interviews, and direct observation) so targets and measurement systems are anchored to real contextual priorities before intervention design scales up.

Habilitation and social validity first: Do not jump straight to treatment because a behavior is noticeable. First decide whether changing it will improve the learner's life over time. Pick targets that matter in real routines, increase useful independence, and are valued by the learner and support network .

Cusp versus pivotal: A behavioral cusp opens access to new environments or opportunities the learner could not reach before. A pivotal behavior produces ripple effects across other skills that were not directly taught. Both are valuable, but they describe different pathways .

Operational definitions for IOA: Define targets so two observers can watch the same moment and score the same event. Use observable wording, concrete boundaries, and examples that make measurement dependable. Anchor selection in records, interviews, and direct observation before treatment scales up .

How to study this chapter for BCBA prep: On every target-selection stem, ask: Does this improve long-term functioning (habilitation)? Do stakeholders value it (social validity)? Is the vignette describing a cusp, a pivotal behavior, or a definition problem?

High-Yield Confusion Points: High-yield confusion points in this chapter include confusing behavioral cusps with pivotal behaviors, selecting convenience targets over habilitation, treating social validity as an afterthought, and writing subjective definitions that fail IOA. Resolve them by checking access expansion versus collateral spread, stakeholder value, and whether the definition is observable before choosing an answer.
