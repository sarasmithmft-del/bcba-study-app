# 07 — Voice & Tone Guide

Your voice is **bold positioning with warm mentorship underneath.** This document makes that abstract idea concrete enough that you (or a ghostwriter, or future team) can stay on-brand without thinking about it.

---

## Voice in One Sentence

> Speak like a hygienist who built a million-dollar chair, sat down next to you in scrubs, and is about to teach you everything she knows — fiercely, warmly, and without apology.

---

## The 5 Voice Rules

### Rule 1 — Bold Claim, Warm Delivery
Always lead with a strong, position-taking statement. Then immediately soften the delivery with personal voice or care for the reader.

> **On-brand**:
> *"Hygiene isn't a cleaning. It's the engine of your entire practice. And if no one's told you that — sit down, take a breath, and let me show you what I mean."*

> **Off-brand (too soft)**:
> *"Hygiene can be a really important part of practice growth, depending on the situation."*

> **Off-brand (too hard)**:
> *"Anyone who treats hygiene as a cleaning is failing as a dentist."*

### Rule 2 — Stories Before Stats
Every concept gets a story first, then the framework, then the numbers. Never lead with a stat.

> **On-brand**:
> *"She walked out without scheduling. Again. I went to the break room and cried into a granola bar. That was the morning I realized I'd been treating my chair like a cleaning station, not a conversation. Hygienists who close that gap raise case acceptance by 20–40 points — but the gap doesn't close with a script. It closes with a shift."*

### Rule 3 — Second Person, Often
Talk *to* the reader, not *about* them. Use "you" and "we." Avoid "the hygienist" and "the doctor" except when explicitly drawing roles.

> **On-brand**:
> *"You know the patient I'm talking about. The one who shows up every six months, opens wide, says 'fine' when you ask how things are, and walks out without saying yes to the crown you've been watching for two years."*

### Rule 4 — First-Person Confession, Often
Show yourself failing. The brand's authority comes from *having been there*, not from never having struggled.

> **On-brand**:
> *"For the first six years of my career, I was terrified to say the word 'cancer' in the chair. Then I missed one. I'm not going to tell you that story to make you feel bad. I'm going to tell you because it's the reason I now teach the Triple Intake."*

### Rule 5 — Name What You Do, Always
Use your proprietary terminology with the ™ symbol on first reference. Train the audience to use your language.

> **On-brand**:
> *"This is what I call **The Trust Window™** — the first 90 seconds of every hygiene appointment, where trust is set or lost for the rest of the visit."*

## Do / Don't Quick Reference

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Use first names ("Maya"), short sentences, contractions | Use "the clinician," "the hygiene professional," "one must" |
| Say "I bombed this" | Say "Mistakes were made" |
| Use specific numbers ("$440k of restorative") | Use vague claims ("a lot of revenue") |
| Address hygienists *and* doctors directly | Speak only to one audience |
| Acknowledge burnout, exhaustion, dental school's gaps | Pretend dentistry is easy or always rewarding |
| Reframe industry truisms ("Stay in your lane" → "Build your lane") | Restate them unchallenged |
| Quote real patients (de-identified) | Use stock-photo patient names like "John Doe" |
| Use sensory detail (the granola bar, the bib clip, the 9pm) | Stay in abstractions |
| Trademark your terms on first use | Bury your IP in jargon |

## Sentence-Level Texture (the "rhythm")

You want a rhythm that feels like a hygienist talking confidently across the room. Three rules of thumb:

1. **Vary length.** Pair a long sentence with a short one. "Then a short one." See?
2. **One-sentence paragraphs are allowed.** They are emphasis on the page.
3. **End chapters on a single question.** It's the page-turn move.

### Sample paragraph in voice

> *Most hygienists I meet are exhausted. Not by the work — they love the work — but by being treated like a tooth janitor in a body that went through two years of school and a board exam to be a clinician. They are the longest, most repeated, most intimate touchpoint a patient has with the practice. And no one ever told them that's where the practice grows.*
>
> *Until now. Until you. Until this book.*
>
> *So put the granola bar down. Open Chapter 1. Let's go to work.*

## Visual Identity (a starting brief for a designer)

Voice has a visual analog. Whoever designs your brand should be given:

- **Photography style**: Real hygienists, real operatories, real patients. **No stock photos. Ever.** Slight grain, natural light, mid-shots over close-ups. Show the hygienist's hands at work, the patient making eye contact.
- **Color palette starter**: A confident, slightly unexpected anchor (deep teal, oxblood, or olive) paired with warm cream and a single accent. Avoid medical pastels and "wellness sage."
- **Type**: A sturdy serif for headlines (you're an authority, not a wellness brand) + a clean modern sans for body. Avoid script fonts.
- **Logo**: Mark + wordmark. The mark could be:
  - A simplified dental chair silhouette
  - A "C" in the chair-shape (works for The Hygiene Engine or The Sacred Chair or Chair Talk)
  - A reclining figure / chair geometric abstraction
- **Tone of design**: 70% editorial / 30% clinical. Looks like a serious book brand, not a clinic brochure.

## Voice Tests Before You Publish

For any piece of writing — chapter, post, email, talk — ask:

1. **Bold-warm test**: Would the boldest sentence stand up at a podium? Would the warmest sentence work in a DM to a struggling hygienist at 9pm?
2. **Story test**: Is there at least one real human in this piece?
3. **Term test**: Did I plant or reinforce at least one proprietary term?
4. **You-test**: Am I talking to a person, not at an industry?
5. **Question-close test**: Does this end on a thought the reader has to finish?
